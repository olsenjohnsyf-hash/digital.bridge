Online safety habits

Online safety means protecting your accounts, your personal information, and the people you communicate with. This page teaches awareness. It does not collect passwords or demonstrate an attack.

Why online safety matters

A careful habit is more useful than fear. Most everyday protection comes from four actions taught in public cybersecurity campaigns: recognize phishing, use strong passwords, turn on multi-factor authentication when it is offered, and keep software updated.

Password security awareness

Do

  • Use a long passphrase that you do not use anywhere else.
  • Store important passphrases in a reputable password manager if you have one.
  • Turn on multi-factor authentication for email and other important accounts.
  • Change a password if you think someone else knows it.

Avoid

  • Reusing one short password on school, email, and shopping accounts.
  • Sharing a password with a friend, even as a favor.
  • Leaving a written password in plain view.
  • Typing a password into a page opened from a surprise message.

NIST Special Publication 800-63B is the U.S. technical guideline agencies use for digital identity, including memorized secrets such as passwords. Beginner advice on this page follows the public message used by cybersecurity awareness programs: long, unique passwords plus an extra login check when available.

Phishing awareness

Phishing is a trick that tries to make you open a harmful link or attachment, or to hand over personal information. Messages may arrive by email, text, social media, or phone. They often pretend to come from a school, bank, delivery company, or friend.

The U.S. Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) tells people to look for urgent language, requests for personal or financial information, shortened web addresses, and addresses that are slightly wrong. Correct grammar is not proof that a message is real, because modern tools can write clean sentences.

Example to study

This is a description of a suspicious text, not a link to open: "Your bank account is locked. Confirm your password in the next 15 minutes." The pressure, the password request, and the unexpected sender are warning signs. A real bank does not need you to send a password by text. Close the message and open the bank's app or type the bank's known address yourself.

Recognizing suspicious links and messages

  • The greeting is vague, or the sender address does not match the organization.
  • The message threatens to close an account, claims you won a prize, or says a package is stuck.
  • A link text says one company, but the address you can see is different.
  • An unexpected attachment asks you to enable editing or macros.
  • A friend sends a strange request for money. Confirm through a second channel you already use, such as a phone call.

Protecting personal information

Some details are especially useful to a thief: government identification numbers, full bank or card numbers, passwords, home address combined with a daily schedule, and copies of identification cards.

Share those only with an organization you contacted yourself, through its official site or office. Do not put them in a public comment, a class chat screenshot, or a form on an unfamiliar site. DigitalBridge does not ask for them.

Safe browsing practices

  1. Keep the browser and operating system updated. Updates repair known weaknesses.
  2. Download apps and programs from the official store or the maker's own site.
  3. Lock the device with a PIN, password, or biometrics.
  4. Be cautious about banking or entering passwords on open public Wi-Fi.
  5. Close accounts and pages when you finish on a shared computer, and do not save the password in that browser.

Social media privacy

  • Review who can see each post: public, friends, or a smaller group.
  • Skip live location posts, school pickup details, and documents that show an identification number.
  • Treat a new follow or message from a stranger as untrusted until you know who it is.
  • Ask before you post someone else's photo or private story.

Cybersecurity awareness

Cybersecurity is the practice of protecting devices, accounts, and information from unauthorized access. You do not need to be a specialist to use the basics. Update software, lock devices, use unique passwords, question surprise messages, and tell a trusted adult when something feels wrong.

The National Institute of Standards and Technology publishes a short public guide on phishing, and the National Cybersecurity Alliance publishes online safety and privacy resources for the public. Both are linked from Community Resources.

Responsible online communication

  • Disagree without insults, threats, or posting someone's private information.
  • Check a shocking claim before you forward it.
  • Give credit when you use another person's words or images.
  • Leave a conversation that becomes abusive and tell a trusted adult if you feel unsafe.

What to do when something looks suspicious

  1. Stop. Do not click, pay, or reply with personal information.
  2. Delete the message. Do not use an unsubscribe link inside a message you already distrust.
  3. If the message might be real, find a contact method you already know. Do not use the phone number or link inside the suspicious message.
  4. Tell a parent, teacher, librarian, or the organization's official support channel.
  5. If you already entered a password, change it from a device you trust and turn on multi-factor authentication if the account offers it.
  6. In the United States, fraud can be reported at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. In other countries, use the official consumer-protection or cybercrime reporting channel where you live. This website does not invent a local hotline.
